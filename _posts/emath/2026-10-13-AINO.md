AI Capability is astonishing and growing. However, its progress scales with verifiability, not understanding, and the hype conflates the two. The blurring of the lines is attribute to many factors and competing interests. My intention is to draw clear lines on the sand.

The training schema of these models are surprisingly simple, yet the resulting systems are very complex. The mistake I’m highlighting is conflating this bounded complexity with human-like comprehension. I believe that these are qualitatively different and no scaling will bridge the gap.

 

At the core of the current ‘AI’ wave are large language models. LLMs are essentially next-token predictors trained at enormous scale. Gazillion parameters trained on massive datasets produced the marvel that was regrettably named ‘AI’ (to clarify, the naming was pre-LLMs, but my point remains as the name carries with it so much science-fiction weight). Recent advances while significant are all engineering advances. It comes from increase in scale, better data, RL on verifiable rewards, test-time compute, and agentic scaffolding. Yet, the core is the same.

 

The astonishing performance of these models shouldn't divert us from their essence. Searle’s Chinese Room hypothetical is an adept analogy. After receiving a prompt, it generates a response using the learned statistical relationships between the tokens.  The output will resemble a thought-out response, but the process itself doesn’t establish any basis for the model to understand what the words refer to.

‘AI’ excels whenever the verification is cheap and scalable (think coding and math proofs). Verifiable datasets let models generate and filter their own training data, and this is where explosive progress is happening. A recent survey of recursive self-improvement research makes a clear distinction between bounded self-improvement where the outputs can be evaluated, and open-ended improvement which to no surprise remains constrained and unreliable unless continuous human intervention and guidance is present in the loop.  https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07663


The over-hype and projecting Ultron-level comprehension is mostly done by CS majors or software engineers (and recently some mathematicians joined the hype train), since their domain is exactly what ‘AI’ is primed for. What they thought of as the pinnacle of intellectual ability, and everything they were taught is now being performed at levels far exceeding expectations. Because software and mathematics offer unusually strong feedback signals, progress there can create the impression that equivalent progress will transfer to other less verifiable and complex domains. As scientists are prone to reducing the world to their specific domain, so do computer scientists.

 

There are two assumptions that are behind their belief, and they are often bundled together. First is their confidence that intellect is purely a computational phenomenon. While this is plausible, it is not as self-evident as they believe. The second is that the current ‘A.I.” has necessary ingredients for such comprehension to emerge. Even if we give the first point, knowing the math and process behind creating LLMs (AI’s core) I find it difficult to agree. LLMs are optimized to predict tokens, which now exists within a broader engineered systems to produce more useful outputs. But nowhere exists any reasonable grounds for comprehension, and no amount of scaling will change this.

 

It doesn’t surprise me that scientists and experts of different domains that have been using machine learning algorithms in their field, understand the limitations of these ‘AI’ models. Which can be attributed to their internal model of the world being more complex than the software developers.  There is no doubt in my mind that ‘AI’ as it is currently will never develop comprehension, let alone gain sentience. My conclusion is not that machine comprehension is impossible in principle, but that scaling the current paradigm will not get there.

 

None of this makes ‘AI’ trivial. it doesn’t need human-like comprehension to disrupt the global economy.  The ability to generate, test and refine at enormous speed will surely raise productivity of some while displacing others. The internet and software economy is being reshaped as we speak, not to mention the enormous cybersecurity threats that’ll surface. We should take this new technology very seriously, but only for the right reasons.  ‘AI’ does not need to understand the world to transform it. But we need to understand that distinction before deciding where and when to trust it.
